A quotation answers one question: what will this cost? For specialist medical devices, surgical implants and procedural consumables, that is rarely the only question that matters. The right product needs to arrive in the right specification, with the right supporting items, in time for the case or the replenishment cycle it was ordered for.

This guide sets out the questions clinical teams, theatre managers and procurement departments can ask before they place an order. It is not clinical guidance; it is about preparing a supply decision.

## Start with the specification, not the price

Two quotations are only comparable when they describe the same thing. Before comparing prices, check that each quotation matches:

- the exact product type and size range your team uses
- the packaging format, including whether items are supplied sterile or non-sterile
- the pack size and unit of issue, so that per-unit costs are compared fairly
- any instruments, trays or accessories the product depends on

A lower line price can disappear quickly if the pack size is different, an essential instrument is quoted separately or the specification is close to, but not quite, what the surgeon asked for.

## Ask about compatibility early

Many implants and consumables are designed to work as part of a system. Screws need the matching instruments; RF electrodes and cannulae need to suit the generator platform in use; cranial fixation components may depend on a particular instrument set or tray.

When you ask for a quotation, say which system or platform you already use, or which one you are considering. A supplier can then confirm what is compatible rather than leaving the question for theatre day.

## Check the documentation your facility needs

Procurement and biomedical engineering teams often need documentation before a product can be evaluated or added to stock. Depending on your facility's process, that may include product information, instructions for use, technical data or supplier credentials.

It helps to state at the start what your process requires. In South Africa, a supplier's SAHPRA establishment licence relates to the company's activities; it is not an approval of each individual product. If your process needs product-level regulatory information, ask for it specifically.

## Plan around the date, not just the order

Specialist supply is usually tied to a date: a planned procedure, a theatre list or a stock replenishment cycle. When you enquire, include the date you are working towards. That gives the supplier a clear basis for confirming what is available and when, and gives your team time to adjust if something needs to change.

For recurring consumables, it can also help to share expected quantities and how often you reorder. Planning replenishment in advance reduces the risk of last-minute substitutions.

## Make the enquiry easy to answer

A clear enquiry usually gets a clearer answer. A useful supply enquiry includes:

1. The speciality and product type
2. The specification, size or system you need
3. The quantity
4. The date you are working towards
5. Any documentation your facility requires
6. The best person to contact, and how

Never include patient names, scans or other patient information in a supply enquiry. Product and supply questions can be answered without them.

## Supplier credentials can form part of the decision

Institutional buyers often weigh supplier credentials alongside product and price, for example industry association membership, licensing, quality certification and B-BBEE status. Ask suppliers to state these accurately and to provide supporting documents where your process requires them.
