The first order for a product gets attention: specifications are checked, samples may be evaluated and someone signs off. The tenth order is often a line on a requisition, written from memory or copied from an old invoice. That is where small differences creep in.

This article looks at why repeat orders go wrong and how wards, theatres and cath labs can make them more reliable. It uses examples from urology consumables and radiation protection gloves. It does not suggest that any two products are interchangeable; that is a decision for your clinical and procurement teams.

## Four ways a repeat order goes wrong

### 1. The description is not specific enough

"Urinary catheter" or "lead gloves, medium" might be clear to the person who wrote it, but not to the person filling the order. For urinary drainage catheters, a usable description normally includes the product type and the specification your ward uses, as listed on the product label or your previous order. For radiation protection gloves, it includes the product, the size and whether it is the same sterile, single-use product you ordered before.

Public buyers deal with this by matching every delivery to a written order. The national surgical instruments contract [RT252-2025][rt252], for example, requires each delivery to carry a delivery note with the official order number, and says deliveries that do not comply with the purchase order will be returned at the supplier's expense. The principle applies to any facility: the order is the reference, so it needs to be precise.

### 2. The pack size or unit of issue is different

"10" can mean ten units or ten boxes. A change in pack size, or in whether the item is priced per unit or per box, is one of the easiest ways for a repeat order to arrive in the wrong quantity, or for a price comparison to mislead. Always state the pack size and the unit you are ordering in.

### 3. The material or specification has changed

Products change: a manufacturer updates a material, a component or the packaging. Sometimes the change matters clinically or to a particular team member, for example where latex sensitivity is a concern. The Western Cape consumables bid [WCGHCC0001/2025][wc1] asks suppliers to confirm in writing that items specified as latex-free are free of latex, and to show this on the packaging. That is a useful reminder to check material statements on repeat orders, not only on the first one.

For radiation protection gloves, sizing and materials are exactly the details to confirm from the product information rather than assume. If anything about the product appears different, ask before the order is confirmed.

### 4. It is not clear whether the order is routine or for a procedure

A ward's regular order of drainage catheters and a theatre's request for an access sheath or retrieval basket for next Tuesday's list are different kinds of request. The first is about keeping stock steady; the second is tied to a date. When the two are mixed in one message, the procedure item can lose its urgency or the stock order can be mistaken for a one-off.

## A simple reorder checklist

Before you send a repeat order, check that it includes:

1. **Product description** as it appears on the label or your previous order
2. **Specification**, for example the size or type your team uses
3. **Pack size and unit of issue** (per item or per box)
4. **Quantity**
5. **Order type**: routine replenishment, or for a specific procedure date
6. **Delivery point** and the person receiving it
7. **Your previous order reference**, if you have one
8. **Your official order number**, where your facility uses one

## When something changes, confirm these

If the supplier tells you an item has changed, or the delivered item looks different, confirm:

- **Product identity:** is it the same product, or a different one being offered as an alternative?
- **Specification:** size, dimensions or type
- **Material:** including any latex statement where relevant
- **Sterility and packaging:** whether it is supplied sterile, and the packaging format
- **Pack size and unit of issue**
- **Labelling:** batch or lot number and expiry date on the packaging

The Western Cape bid's packaging requirements are a useful model of what a label should show: manufacturer or supplier name, item name and code, date of manufacture, expiry date, batch or lot number and, for sterile items, the sterilisation date, expiry and method ([WCGHCC0001/2025][wc1], section 3.6).

A different product is not automatically equivalent. If an alternative is proposed, it should go through whatever evaluation your facility requires.

## Separate stock orders from procedure requests

Where you can, send routine replenishment and procedure-specific requests separately, and say which is which. For a procedure request, include the date. For routine stock, it can help to share how often you usually reorder, so the supplier can plan.
