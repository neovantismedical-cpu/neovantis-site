Ask four people in the same hospital what they need from a medical device supplier and you will get four different answers. The surgeon wants to know that the right implant and the instruments to place it will be there on the day. The theatre coordinator wants to know when the set will arrive and who to phone when the list changes. Procurement wants accurate documents, a quote with a clear scope and an order process that works the second time as well as the first. The clinical engineering team wants to know who will maintain the equipment once the warranty ends.

None of these questions is new, and a good deal of it is written down. South African public-sector tender documents, sterile services procedures and regulatory guidance spell out much of what suppliers are expected to provide. This article looks at a selection of those documents, explains what each one shows, and is clear about where its relevance ends.

## What this article draws on, and its limits

We reviewed recent publicly available documents rather than surveys or interviews:

- two National Treasury transversal contract documents: [RT2-2025][rt2] (medical equipment) and [RT252-2025][rt252] (surgical instruments and equipment)
- two Western Cape Department of Health and Wellness bid documents: [WCGHCC0001/2025][wc1] (prosthetic and orthotic consumables) and [WCGHCC0047/2025][wc47] (a specialised 3D printer for the Orthotic and Prosthetic Centre)
- the [CSSD Forums of South Africa (CFSA) Standard Operating Procedures, version 3 (2024)][cfsa], which state that they are referred to in the Office of Health Standards Compliance's CSSD audit tool
- SAHPRA's [licensing guideline][sahpra-md07] and a [2025 communication to industry][sahpra-md08]

Three cautions apply throughout.

- **These are specific documents, not national rules.** A tender sets requirements for one contract. Another province, another contract or a private hospital group may ask for something different.
- **Most are public-sector documents.** Private hospital groups set their own supplier requirements, and those are generally not published.
- **The products differ.** A 3D printer for an orthotic centre and a surgical instrument contract are not the same as an implant system or an RF generator. They show the kinds of questions buyers ask, not the exact terms for every product.

We have separated what the documents **require** from the questions a clinical team **might reasonably ask**. The second list is our suggestion, not a finding.

## The surgeon: will everything I need be there for this case?

For a planned case, the surgeon's question is not only "is the implant available?". An implant is only useful with the instruments needed to place it, and those instruments may arrive as a loan set that has to be cleaned and sterilised at the hospital before it can go to theatre.

**What the documents show.** The CFSA SOP on managing loan sets lists the consequences of getting this wrong in plain terms. It says cancellation, delay or prolonged surgical procedures can result from loan sets not being ordered, or from "damaged incorrect or missing instruments on sets", and it records late delivery as a risk because it can lead to "reprocessing short cuts" ([CFSA SOP No. 28][cfsa]). The same SOPs say that individual loan sets must be ordered for each patient for individual procedures.

**What it shows, and its limits.** The SOPs set out good practice for sterile services departments. They do not tell you how often cases are delayed in South Africa, and they do not set supplier contract terms on their own. They do show that a surgeon's confidence depends on the instrument set as much as on the implant.

**Questions a surgeon might reasonably ask a supplier:**

- Is the system I have in mind available, and are the matching instruments available for the same date?
- If the instruments come as a loan set, when will the set reach our hospital?
- If the planned date moves, who confirms that the implants and set can move with it?

## The theatre coordinator and CSSD: when will it arrive, and who do I call?

For the theatre coordinator and the central sterile services department (CSSD), the questions are about timing, completeness and contacts.

**What the documents show.** The CFSA SOP on loaner medical devices ([SOP No. 7][cfsa]) sets out specific expectations:

- the CSSD must be told the date of the booked surgery, the doctor, the procedure and the type of loan equipment **at least a day before delivery**, with estimated delivery, use and return times
- loan sets should reach the decontamination area "at a very minimum 4 hours before use", with more time needed when many trays have to be processed
- the loan set company should provide the number of trays, details of the prostheses supplied, the date of use, cleaning and sterilising instructions, tray checklists that unfamiliar staff can follow, and photographs to help with checking
- the supplier is responsible for training staff on disassembly and decontamination of its items
- the facility should have a written agreement with the lender

**What it shows, and its limits.** Four hours is a stated *minimum* for decontamination, not a recommended delivery time; the SOP itself says more time is needed for larger sets. Individual hospitals set their own cut-off times, often earlier. The SOP shows that a supplier's job is not finished when a set is dispatched: the paperwork and checklists that travel with it matter to the people who process it.

**Questions a theatre coordinator might reasonably ask:**

- What time will the set arrive, and does that leave our CSSD enough time?
- Will the set come with a tray list and photographs that match what is in the trays?
- Who is the contact person if the list changes, the case is cancelled, or something is missing when the set is checked?

## Procurement: are the documents right, and will the reorder work?

Procurement teams need to know that a supplier and its products meet the facility's requirements, that the quote describes what will actually be delivered, and that repeat orders will arrive as specified.

**What the documents show.** The Western Cape consumables bid [WCGHCC0001/2025][wc1] requires bidders to provide:

- a valid ISO 13485 certificate for **each manufacturer** whose products are offered
- a certified copy of a SAHPRA licence as a manufacturer, distributor or wholesaler of medical devices
- where the bidder is not the manufacturer, written proof that the manufacturer has no objection to the offer and will continue to supply for the contract period
- latex-free confirmation for items specified to be free of latex
- samples for clinical evaluation, and packaging that shows the manufacturer, item name and code, batch or lot number, expiry date and, for sterile items, the sterilisation date, expiry and method

The same bid states that the department may award an item to more than one supplier "to address item availability and compatibility".

The national surgical instruments contract [RT252-2025][rt252] adds order discipline. It says quantities are ordered "as and when required" with no guarantee of volume. Each delivery must carry a delivery note with the official order number, and deliveries that do not comply with the purchase order "will be returned to the Supplier(s) at the Supplier's expense". It also asks for brand names and sizes to be engraved on certain instruments, or the original packaging to be supplied.

**What it shows, and its limits.** These are state tenders for specific product categories. They show the level of documentation public buyers expect at tender stage. A private hospital's onboarding may ask for less, more or different documents. Notably, both documents ask for evidence about the **manufacturer** (ISO 13485) and about the **supplier** (SAHPRA licence) separately.

**Questions a buyer might reasonably ask:**

- Does the quote state the exact product, pack size and unit of issue, and what is excluded?
- Which documents can you provide for the supplier, and which for each product?
- If the manufacturer, material or pack size changes during the contract, how will we be told?

## Clinical and biomedical engineering: who maintains this equipment?

For equipment such as generators, the clinical engineering team's question is about the years after installation.

**What the documents show.** The national medical equipment contract [RT2-2025][rt2] is explicit. National Treasury lists it for the period 1 August 2026 to 31 July 2030 ([transversal contracts list][nt-list]). It requires:

- commissioning that includes practical training for clinical and technical staff, and handover of catalogues, operating manuals and instruction leaflets
- formal first-line technical maintenance training for the institution's technical staff, and user training for clinical staff, at no extra cost
- user-care information from the manufacturer or supplier, such as cleaning, disinfection or sterilisation methods for reusable equipment
- a loan device where equipment under warranty needs prolonged corrective maintenance
- stated call-out response times, and a call centre for logging calls
- continued liability for maintenance and after-sales support of delivered items; if a model is discontinued, supporting documents from the manufacturer and spare parts guaranteed for at least seven years
- prompt notification of recalls or alerts on awarded items

The Western Cape equipment bid [WCGHCC0047/2025][wc47] asks bidders to name their technical staff in South Africa with competency certificates. It also asks bidders to say which consumables the equipment needs and which are proprietary, to quote planned maintenance costs year by year for at least five years, to guarantee spares for ten years, and to supply detailed preventive maintenance and calibration procedures and service manuals. It states that the equipment will be maintained by hospital technical staff after the guarantee period.

**What it shows, and its limits.** These requirements apply to equipment bought under those contracts. They are a useful indication of what clinical engineering teams expect to discuss, but each supplier's arrangements depend on the equipment and the manufacturer. Training, maintenance and support should be confirmed in writing for the specific device, not assumed.

**Questions a clinical engineering team might reasonably ask:**

- Who maintains the equipment during and after warranty: the supplier, the manufacturer or our own team?
- Which consumables and accessories does it need, and are any proprietary?
- What training is offered for users and for technical staff, and who provides it?
- Which manuals and procedures will we receive?

## Regulation: what a licence does and does not tell you

Many of these documents ask for a SAHPRA licence. It helps to be precise about what that licence covers.

SAHPRA issues three types of medical device establishment licence: manufacturer, distributor and wholesaler ([SAHPRA licensing guideline SAHPGL-MD-07, May 2025][sahpra-md07]). Applicants list the devices they manufacture, distribute or wholesale as part of the application ([SAHPRA medical devices page][sahpra-md]). In a 2025 communication, SAHPRA stated that it "has not yet called up medical devices … for product registration" ([SAHPRA MD08, 2025][sahpra-md08]). The RT2-2025 contract anticipates this changing: once devices are called up for registration by notice in the Government Gazette, it will require product registration certificates too.

In practice, a supplier's establishment licence tells you about the company's authorised activities. It is not, on its own, an approval of each product. Buyers can check whether a company holds a licence on SAHPRA's "Medical Devices Licences Issued" database.

## What this means for suppliers

Across these documents, the same themes recur:

| Who is asking | Documented example | What it points to |
|---|---|---|
| Surgeon | CFSA: delays and cancellations when loan sets are not ordered or are incomplete | Confirm instruments as well as implants, for the same date |
| Theatre and CSSD | CFSA: notice at least a day before; delivery at least 4 hours before use; tray lists and photos | Timing, complete sets and a named contact |
| Procurement | WC 0001/2025 and RT252-2025: manufacturer ISO 13485, supplier SAHPRA licence, labelling, orders matched to delivery | Accurate documents and repeatable orders |
| Clinical engineering | RT2-2025 and WC 0047/2025: training, response times, manuals, spares, after-sales support | Clear maintenance responsibility over the equipment's life |

The common thread is clarity. Clinical teams are not only asking whether a product exists, but whether the supplier can tell them exactly what will arrive, when, with what documents, and who is responsible afterwards.

## A checklist for your next supplier conversation

Use or adapt these questions:

1. Is the product available for our date, and are the matching instruments, trays or accessories available for the same date?
2. If a loan set is involved, when will it arrive, and will it come with tray lists, photographs and reprocessing instructions?
3. Who is our contact if the date, the list or the requirements change?
4. Does the quote state the exact product, specification, pack size and unit of issue, and what is excluded?
5. Which supplier documents (licence, certification) and which product documents (product information, instructions for use, labelling) can you provide?
6. How will we be told if a product, material, pack size or manufacturer changes?
7. For equipment: who maintains it, what training is included, which consumables does it need, and which manuals will we receive?
