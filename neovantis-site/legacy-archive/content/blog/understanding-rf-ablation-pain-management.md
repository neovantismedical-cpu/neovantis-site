## What Is Radiofrequency Ablation?

Radiofrequency (RF) ablation is a minimally invasive treatment approach used in specialist pain management to target pain-transmitting nerves with controlled energy delivery. Rather than masking pain through ongoing pharmacological management, RF ablation addresses pain at a neural level — making it a meaningful option for appropriately selected patients with chronic pain conditions that have not responded sufficiently to conservative management.

The procedure involves the placement of a specialised electrode near the target nerve, guided by imaging, through which a controlled radiofrequency current is delivered. The energy generated at the electrode tip disrupts the nerve's ability to transmit pain signals, providing targeted relief that is not dependent on ongoing medication.

RF ablation is performed as a day procedure in specialist clinical settings — including hospital-based interventional suites, specialist pain clinics, and anaesthetic procedure environments — by trained pain management specialists, anaesthesiologists, and interventional practitioners.

## How RF Ablation Works

At its core, RF ablation relies on the application of controlled radiofrequency energy to a target neural structure. The current generates localised heat at the electrode tip, which — when applied at the right temperature and duration — creates a lesion that interrupts the nerve's pain-conducting function.

The precision of the procedure is a defining feature. Because the target is a specific nerve or nerve branch, the treatment is focused rather than systemic. This specificity differentiates RF ablation from broader pharmacological pain management strategies and is central to why it is valued in interventional pain practice.

### Two Primary Treatment Approaches

**Conventional Radiofrequency Ablation** involves continuous energy delivery to create a sustained thermal lesion at the target nerve. This approach is commonly used when the clinical target can be precisely defined and a controlled lesion is appropriate — most frequently in spinal and facet joint-related pain pathways.

**Pulsed Radiofrequency** delivers short bursts of high-voltage RF energy with cooling intervals between pulses. Because this approach avoids sustained thermal lesion formation, it is used near nerve structures where avoiding thermal injury is a clinical priority — such as certain peripheral nerve and dorsal root ganglion applications. Pulsed RF is a neuromodulatory technique with a different mechanism profile to conventional ablation.

The availability of both modalities from a single RF system gives the treating clinician flexibility in tailoring the treatment approach to the specific anatomy and clinical requirements of each patient.

## Why Precision Matters

In interventional pain management, precision is not simply a technical preference — it is a clinical requirement. The proximity of target nerves to adjacent anatomical structures means that accurate electrode placement and controlled energy delivery are fundamental to both clinical effectiveness and procedural safety.

Precision in RF ablation relates to several dimensions of the procedure:

- **Imaging guidance**: RF procedures are typically performed under fluoroscopic or ultrasound guidance to confirm accurate electrode placement before energy is delivered.
- **Equipment quality**: The reliability and accuracy of the RF generator, electrode system, and temperature sensing components directly influence the consistency of the thermal lesion produced.
- **Patient selection**: Appropriate identification of candidates — including diagnostic nerve block testing prior to ablation — is a prerequisite for achieving meaningful outcomes.

When these elements are aligned, RF ablation can provide durable, clinically meaningful pain relief in well-selected patients.

## Broad Clinical Application

RF ablation is used across a range of specialist pain management applications where minimally invasive, targeted neural intervention is clinically indicated. While specific clinical decision-making rests with the treating practitioner and their assessment of each patient, the technique has established relevance across spinal pain pathways, joint-related pain management, and selected peripheral nerve applications.

The minimally invasive nature of the procedure — typically performed as a day case with a short recovery profile — is a practical advantage in healthcare environments where patient throughput, comfort, and recovery time are priorities alongside clinical outcomes.

## Duration and Sustainability of Effect

One of the clinically valued characteristics of RF ablation is the durability of its effects. Because the procedure physically alters the nerve's conduction function rather than relying on ongoing drug delivery, the pain relief it provides is not subject to pharmacological tolerance and is not dependent on patient compliance with ongoing medication.

The duration of effect varies between patients and procedural contexts, but meaningful relief lasting many months is achievable in appropriately selected patients. In cases where pain returns over time — due to nerve regeneration — retreatment is generally feasible and can produce equivalent results to the initial procedure.

This sustainability supports RF ablation as a long-term pain management strategy rather than a one-time intervention, with relevance across both acute management phases and ongoing chronic pain programmes.
